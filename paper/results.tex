\section{Results}

We report on two datasets. UCI-HAR is primary: clients are the individual
volunteers the data was collected from, so the client boundary is a real
person rather than a synthetic partition, and the task is the sensor-on-edge
setting the study is about. CIFAR-10 is secondary and checks the same effects
on a harder benchmark.

All figures are means over three seeds. The measurement noise floor, computed
from the across-seed spread within each condition, is $\pm 5.43$~W ($9.46\%$)
on HAR and $\pm 4.38$~W ($6.38\%$) on CIFAR-10; no difference smaller than
that is treated as resolvable. HAR rounds are short (about $0.48$~s), so
per-round energy there is a noisier estimate than on CIFAR-10 and whole-run
totals are the more reliable quantity.

\subsection{The Per-Round Cost of Privacy Is Fixed}

Table~\ref{tab:perround} gives energy per round on HAR. Net of the idle
baseline, private training costs $21.0$~J per round on average against
$14.5$~J without privacy, an overhead of $+45\%$. Across the five private
conditions the per-round figure varies by $8.2\%$ of its mean against a
$9.46\%$ noise floor, so within the resolution of the instrument the
per-round cost does not depend on $\varepsilon$. CIFAR-10 agrees: $+60\%$
overhead, $9.6\%$ spread against a $6.38\%$ floor.

Wall-clock says the same thing more robustly. Every private condition takes
$47.8$--$48.3$~s for its 100 rounds while the non-private baseline takes
$37.2$~s: a $1.2\%$ spread across private conditions against an $11$~s step up
from $\varepsilon=\infty$. Executed optimiser steps are identical across all
six conditions ($89$ per round), so this is not a workload difference.

The two mechanisms by which privacy consumes energy are therefore separable.
The per-round overhead is an implementation cost --- per-sample gradient
computation and clipping --- paid identically whether the budget is tight or
loose. Everything that depends on $\varepsilon$ acts through the number of
rounds needed, which the next result quantifies. A fixed overhead is addressed
by optimising the DP implementation; a convergence penalty is addressed by the
round budget. Reporting only energy per round would show privacy costing a
flat $45\%$ and would miss the larger effect entirely.

\subsection{The Cost of Privacy Grows with the Target}

This is the central result. Energy per round makes privacy look like a flat
$45\%$ surcharge. Measured against what a deployment actually wants --- a
model of a given quality --- the cost is not flat at all: it is negligible for
an undemanding target, several-fold for a demanding one, and unbounded beyond
a threshold that depends on the privacy budget.

Table~\ref{tab:target} gives energy to target on HAR. At $0.40$ accuracy every
condition costs the same, $178$--$218$~J net, all within the noise floor:
privacy is free at this quality because the target is reached before noise
matters. At $0.50$ the private conditions cost $1.17$--$2.10\times$ the
non-private $204$~J. At $0.70$ the surviving conditions cost
$\mathbf{5.8\times}$ ($1753$~J at $\varepsilon=4$ against $300$~J), and
$\varepsilon \le 2$ never reaches it. At $0.85$ --- which the non-private
model reaches in $57.7$ rounds and $878$~J --- \textbf{no privacy budget we
tested reaches it at any energy expenditure}.

Unreachability is the sharpest form of the cost and is itself a result. A
deployment that fixes a privacy budget is not merely paying more joules for a
given accuracy; above a budget-dependent ceiling it cannot buy that accuracy
at all, because the training that would close the gap is what raises $\sigma$.
Reporting only the configurations that reach a target would hide exactly the
regime that matters most to a practitioner choosing $\varepsilon$.

\subsection{The Energy-Optimal Stopping Round}

Under a sufficiently tight budget the accuracy curve peaks and then declines,
so training past the peak spends energy and loses accuracy at once. On HAR
this appears at $\varepsilon=0.5$ and only there: the model peaks at $0.4712$
at round 17 and ends at $0.4553$, with the following 83 rounds accounting for
$2477$~J, or $83\%$ of the run's energy, and returning nothing. At
$\varepsilon \ge 1$ the HAR curves are still flat or rising at round 100
(Table~\ref{tab:stopping}), so no stopping round is identified for them within
this budget.

CIFAR-10, at 30 rounds, shows the effect across a wider range of budgets, with
the peak round ordering monotonically: 7 at $\varepsilon=0.5$, then 11, 19, 24,
and no peak before round 30 at $\varepsilon \ge 8$. At $\varepsilon=0.5$ the
model reaches $0.169$ at round 7 and ends at $0.161$, the following 23 rounds
accounting for $77\%$ of the run.

The two datasets therefore bound the effect rather than replicating it
identically. The mechanism is the same in both --- accumulated noise
eventually dominates the aggregated update --- but where the crossover falls
relative to the round budget depends on the task. We state the finding as
conditional: under local DP a tight budget induces an energy-optimal stopping
round well before the budget expires, and a deployment that trains for a fixed
round count regardless of $\varepsilon$ risks both burning energy and
degrading the model. We do not claim the monotone ordering of peak rounds
generalises, since HAR does not reproduce it.

\subsection{CIFAR-10 as a Secondary Check}

CIFAR-10 over 30 rounds reproduces the per-round result ($+60\%$ net of idle,
$121$~J against $75.8$~J, flat in $\varepsilon$ to within $9.6\%$ against a
$6.38\%$ floor) and gives the stopping effect across more budgets, as above.
Reaching $0.20$ accuracy costs $1482$~J net at $\varepsilon=1$ against $406$~J
without privacy, $3.65\times$ for the same accuracy, because the private run
needs $11.7$ rounds where the non-private run needs $2.3$; at
$\varepsilon=0.5$ that target is never reached.

Absolute accuracies are low on this benchmark --- $0.503$ without privacy and
$0.16$--$0.31$ under it --- which is expected for a $62$k-parameter CNN over 30
non-IID rounds, and is why HAR carries the headline claims. What matters here
is that both datasets agree on the shape of both effects.

\subsection{Ablations}

We vary one factor at a time at $\varepsilon \in \{1, \infty\}$, holding the
others at the configuration of Section~III, with three seeds per cell. On HAR
only local epochs and the clipping norm are meaningful: a client there is a
study participant, so the federation size is fixed by the split and there is
no Dirichlet parameter to vary.

\textbf{Under a tight budget the model becomes noise-limited.} This is the
clearest reading of the HAR ablations and it is a negative result worth
stating. At $\varepsilon=1$, neither factor moves accuracy: final accuracy
spans $0.5427$--$0.5573$ across one, two and five local epochs ($2.6\%$ of the
mean) and $0.5534$--$0.5660$ across $C \in \{0.5, 1, 2\}$ ($2.3\%$), both
comfortably inside the $9.46\%$ noise floor. With $\sigma$ between $9.2$ and
$10.9$, the injected noise dominates whatever these settings contribute, and
tuning them is not a route to a better private model.

\textbf{The standard efficiency lever stops paying under privacy.} Increasing
local work per round is how federated learning ordinarily buys fewer
communication rounds, and on HAR without privacy it works: energy to reach
$0.70$ accuracy falls from $304$~J at one local epoch to $192$~J at five, a
$1.6\times$ saving, while accuracy is unchanged within noise. Under
$\varepsilon=1$ that saving disappears entirely --- the target is not reached
at any epoch count --- while energy per round still rises from $29$~J to
$64$~J, a factor of $2.2$. The practitioner pays the compute and receives
nothing, because local steps enter $s_{\text{total}}$: more local work per
round consumes budget faster and forces a larger $\sigma$ for the same
$\varepsilon$, cancelling the convergence gain it would otherwise buy.

\textbf{On a harder task the lever inverts rather than merely failing.} The
CIFAR-10 ablations, where the private model operates much closer to chance,
show the same mechanism pushed further. At $\varepsilon=1$ accuracy falls from
$0.178$ to $0.134$ to $0.101$ across one, two and five local epochs, a $56\%
$ spread against a $6.38\%$ floor, the peak round collapses from 13 to 6 to 4,
and energy per round rises from $153$~J to $570$~J. The two costs compound: a
configuration that spends more per round also reaches its own peak sooner, so
energy spent after the peak grows from $2596$~J to $14819$~J, and at five
epochs the $0.20$ target is never reached at all.

\textbf{Clipping is free in compute and matters only where the model is not
noise-limited.} On both datasets $C$ leaves energy per round unchanged
($29$~J across all three values on HAR; $144$--$183$~J on CIFAR-10, inside the
floor), so it acts purely on convergence. On HAR it does not act at all, for
the reason above. On CIFAR-10 it acts strongly and in a way that final
accuracy alone hides: $C=2$ peaks at $0.191$ at round 8 and ends at $0.102$,
giving back $46.5\%$ of its peak, against $3.1\%$ at $C=0.5$. Loose clipping
therefore reaches nearly the same quality as tight clipping and then loses it,
which is only visible if the peak is reported alongside the final value. Our
fixed $C=1.0$ is a conservative choice rather than an optimal one, and on
CIFAR-10 every private result would improve at $C=0.5$.

Taken together the ablations bound where hyperparameter tuning helps. Where
the private model retains useful signal, as on CIFAR-10, the settings matter
and can be got badly wrong. Where the noise dominates, as on HAR at
$\varepsilon=1$, they do not matter at all, and the only levers left are the
privacy budget and the round budget.

\subsection{Carbon Cost by Grid}

Identical workloads emit different amounts of CO$_2$ depending on where they
run. Table~\ref{tab:carbon} scales the measured HAR energy to 1000 training
runs and applies published grid carbon intensities. The scaling repeats the
measured workload; it is not an extrapolation to different hardware.

The cost attributable to privacy --- private minus non-private --- ranges from
$105$ to $133$~gCO$_2$eq per 1000 runs on Algeria's gas-dominated grid, against
$5.6$ to $7.1$~g in Norway: a factor of $18.8$ for the same computation and the
same guarantee. Choosing a privacy budget is therefore in part a geographic
decision. The same $\varepsilon$, bought with the same joules, carries an
environmental price that differs by more than an order of magnitude across
deployment regions, which is a consideration absent from the privacy--utility
framing the literature uses.
