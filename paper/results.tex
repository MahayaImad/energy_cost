\section{Results}

All figures below are means over three seeds. The measurement noise floor,
computed from the across-seed spread within each condition, is $\pm 4.38$~W
($6.38\%$); we treat no difference smaller than this as resolvable.

\subsection{The Per-Round Cost of Privacy Is Fixed}

Table~\ref{tab:perround} gives energy per round for each budget. Net of the
idle baseline, differentially private training costs $121$~J per round on
average against $75.8$~J without privacy, an overhead of $+60\%$. Across the
five private conditions the per-round figure varies by $9.6\%$ of its mean,
against the $6.38\%$ noise floor, so within the resolution of the instrument
the per-round cost does not depend on $\varepsilon$.

This separates the two mechanisms by which privacy consumes energy. The
per-round overhead is an implementation cost --- per-sample gradient
computation and clipping --- and is paid identically whether the budget is
tight or loose. Everything that depends on $\varepsilon$ must therefore act
through the number of rounds, which is what the remaining results measure.

The engineering implications differ. A fixed overhead is addressed by
optimising the DP implementation; a convergence penalty is addressed by
changing the round budget or the stopping rule. Reporting only energy per
round would show privacy costing a flat $60\%$ and would miss the larger
effect entirely.

\subsection{Energy to Target Accuracy}

Energy per round makes privacy look cheap. The quantity that matters to a
battery-powered deployment is the energy required to reach a usable model,
and Table~\ref{tab:target} reports it.

At a target of $0.20$ central accuracy, $\varepsilon=1$ requires $1482$~J
(net) against $406$~J without privacy: \textbf{$3.65\times$ the energy for the
same accuracy}. The gap is nearly three times the $+60\%$ per-round overhead,
because the private run needs $11.7$ rounds where the non-private run needs
$2.3$. At $\varepsilon=0.5$ the target is never reached within the 30-round
budget at all.

Unreachability is itself a result. A deployment that fixes both a privacy
budget and a round budget may be unable to produce a model of the required
quality at any energy expenditure, and no amount of additional training within
that budget will change this. We therefore report unreached targets explicitly
rather than omitting those configurations.

The $0.15$ target is reached by every condition within four to six rounds,
where the accuracy curves are still steep and the ordering across $\varepsilon$
is not stable across seeds. We do not draw conclusions from it.

\subsection{An Energy-Optimal Stopping Round}

Under a tight budget the accuracy curve peaks and then declines
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}). Table~\ref{tab:stopping} locates the peak for
each condition. The peak round increases monotonically with the privacy
budget: round 7 at $\varepsilon=0.5$, then 11, 19, 24, and no peak before
round 30 at $\varepsilon=8$.

Past that round, training consumes energy and loses accuracy at the same time.
At $\varepsilon=0.5$ the model reaches $0.169$ at round 7 and ends at $0.161$;
the 23 rounds that follow account for $3866$~J, or $77\%$ of the run's total
energy, and return nothing. The mechanism is that accumulated noise comes to
dominate the aggregated signal once the per-round update carries less
information than the noise added to protect it.

This gives a concrete rule: under local DP the round budget should be chosen
from the privacy budget, and training past the resulting round is doubly
wasteful. Standard practice of training for a fixed round count regardless of
$\varepsilon$ both burns energy and degrades the model.

\subsection{Ablations}

We vary one factor at a time at $\varepsilon \in \{1, \infty\}$, holding the
others at the configuration of Section~III. Results are means over three
seeds.

\textbf{Local epochs invert under privacy.} Increasing local work per round is
the standard way to reduce communication rounds in federated learning. Without
privacy it is close to accuracy-neutral here ($0.514$, $0.552$, $0.525$ for
one, two and five local epochs) while raising energy per round by $3.5\times$.
Under $\varepsilon=1$ it is harmful on both axes: accuracy falls from $0.181$
to $0.129$ to $0.101$, the peak round collapses from 13 to 6 to 3, and energy
per round rises from $164$~J to $585$~J. The accounting model explains this
directly: local steps enter $s_{\text{total}}$, so additional local epochs
consume budget faster and force a larger $\sigma$ for the same
$\varepsilon$. The two costs compound, since a configuration that spends more
per round also reaches its own peak sooner.

\textbf{Clipping norm trades accuracy against target.} At $\varepsilon=1$,
energy per round is unchanged by $C$ ($167$, $164$, $165$~J for $C \in
\{0.5, 1, 2\}$, well within the noise floor), so $C$ acts purely on
convergence. Tighter clipping gives higher final accuracy ($0.206$ against
$0.117$ at $C=2$) and a later peak (round 18 against 6). The cheapest route to
a \emph{low} target is loose clipping --- $C=2$ reaches $0.20$ in $940$~J
against $1725$~J at $C=0.5$ --- but it then collapses, so that figure is only
meaningful together with the stopping round. The optimal clipping norm depends
on the accuracy target, and our fixed $C=1.0$ is a conservative choice rather
than an optimal one.

\textbf{Heterogeneity is masked by noise.} Without privacy, partition skew
behaves as expected: accuracy $0.397$, $0.509$, $0.547$ for $\alpha \in \{0.1,
0.5, 10\}$, and reaching $0.20$ costs $1.5\times$ more energy at $\alpha=0.1$
than at $\alpha=10$. Under $\varepsilon=1$ the ordering is not stable across
seeds, which we read as DP noise dominating the effect of heterogeneity rather
than as evidence of a direction.

\subsection{Carbon Cost by Grid}

Identical workloads emit different amounts of CO$_2$ depending on where they
run. Table~\ref{tab:carbon} scales the measured energy to 1000 training runs
and applies published grid carbon intensities. The scaling is a repetition of
the measured workload, not an extrapolation to different hardware.

The cost attributable to privacy --- the difference between private and
non-private training --- is $228$~gCO$_2$eq per 1000 runs on Algeria's
gas-dominated grid against $12$~g in Norway, a factor of $18.8$ for the same
computation and the same privacy guarantee. Choosing a privacy budget is
therefore in part a geographic decision: the carbon a given $\varepsilon$ costs
depends on where the training happens, and identical privacy requirements
carry very different environmental prices across deployment regions.
