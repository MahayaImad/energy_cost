\section{Results}
\label{sec:Results}

All figures are over three seeds, against noise floors of $7.8\%$ on HAR and $6.38\%$ on CIFAR-10; no smaller difference is treated as resolvable.

\subsection{The Per-Round Cost Is Fixed}

% On HAR, private training consumes $21.0$J per round compared with $14.0$J without privacy, an overhead of $+51\%$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:perround}). CIFAR-10 shows the same pattern, with a $60\%$ overhead and a $9.6\%$ spread. Wall-clock time is similarly stable: private runs take $47.8$--$48.3$~s for 100 rounds, compared with $37.2$~s without privacy.

On HAR, private training consumes $21.0$~J per round against $14.0$~J without privacy, an overhead of $+51\%$ 
(Table~\ref{tab:perround}, Fig.~\ref{fig:perround}). The overhead combines $+26.8\%$ wall-clock and
$+19.0\%$ mean power, so a power ratio alone would report $19\%$. Across the five private budgets the spread is $12.5\%$ of the mean against a $7.8\%$ noise floor, and it does not order with $\varepsilon$: we claim no
systematic dependence rather than equality. CIFAR-10 shows the same pattern ($+60\%$, spread $9.6\%$).

These results separate the two sources of energy cost. The per-round overhead comes from DP operations such as per-sample gradients and clipping, and shows no systematic dependence on $\varepsilon$. In contrast, the $\varepsilon$-dependent cost arises from the number of rounds required to reach a target accuracy. Thus, per-round energy alone captures only the fixed overhead and misses the larger convergence cost.

\subsection{The Cost Grows with the Target}

Energy per round suggests that privacy adds a fixed cost, but the total cost rises sharply with the target accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:target}). Every entry in Table~\ref{tab:target} was reached by all three seeds. At $0.40$ accuracy, privacy adds no measurable cost for $\varepsilon \geq 1$ ($187$--$210$ J against $185$ J without it). The private runs reach this first target in fewer rounds than the non-private one, which we attribute to noise acting as regularization early in training; the effect reverses at every higher target.

At $0.50$, private training requires $1.28$--$1.79\times$ the non-private energy, rising to $5.7\times$ at $0.70$; $\varepsilon \le 2$ does not reach this target. At $0.85$, the non-private model reaches the target in $57.0$ rounds and $839$~J, while no tested private budget reaches it.

Thus, privacy can impose a hard accuracy ceiling: beyond it, additional training cannot recover the target because the extra rounds increase the noise.

\subsection{An Energy-Optimal Stopping Round}

Under a sufficiently tight budget the accuracy curve peaks and declines
(Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}), so training past the peak spends energy and
loses accuracy at once. On HAR this
appears at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ and only there: the model peaks at $0.4712$ at
round 17 and ends at $0.4553$, the following 83 rounds accounting for
$2477$~J, or $83\%$ of the run. At $\varepsilon \ge 1$ the HAR curves are
still flat or rising at round 100.

CIFAR-10, over 30 rounds, shows the effect across more budgets, the peak round
ordering monotonically: 7 at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, then 11, 19, 24, and no peak
before round 30 at $\varepsilon \ge 8$. At $\varepsilon = 0.5$ the model
reaches $0.169$ at round 7 and ends at $0.161$, the remaining rounds
accounting for $77\%$ of the run.

The datasets bound the effect rather than replicating it: the mechanism is the
same—accumulated noise eventually dominates the aggregated update—but
where the crossover falls depends on the task. We state the finding
conditionally. A tight budget induces an energy-optimal stopping round well
before the budget expires, and training for a fixed round count regardless of
$\varepsilon$ risks burning energy while degrading the model; we do not claim
the monotone ordering generalizes, since HAR does not reproduce it.

\subsection{CIFAR-10 as a Secondary Check}

CIFAR-10 reproduces the per-round result ($+60\%$ net of idle, flat to within
$9.6\%$) and gives the stopping effect across more budgets. Reaching $0.20$
accuracy costs $1482$~J net at $\varepsilon = 1$ against $406$~J without
privacy, $3.65\times$, because the private run needs $11.7$ rounds against
$2.3$; at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ it is never reached. Absolute accuracies are low
($0.503$ without privacy, $0.16$--$0.31$ under it), as expected for a $62$k
CNN over 30 non-IID rounds, which is why HAR carries the headline claims.

\subsection{Ablations}

We vary one factor at a time at 	$\varepsilon \in \{1,\infty\}$, using three seeds per setting. On HAR, only local epochs and the clipping norm are varied, as the federation size is fixed by the data split and no Dirichlet parameter is used.

\textbf{Under a tight budget, the model becomes noise-limited.} At $\varepsilon=1$ on HAR, neither local epochs nor clipping norm changes accuracy beyond the $7.8\%$ noise floor. With $\sigma\approx10$, injected noise dominating these settings, tuning them does not improve the private model.

\textbf{The usual efficiency lever stops paying.} Without privacy, increasing local epochs from one to five reduces the energy needed to reach $0.70$ accuracy from $304$~J to $192$~J, a $1.6\times$ saving. At $\varepsilon=1$, however, the target is not reached at any epoch count, while per-round energy increases from $29$~J to $64$~J. More local steps increase $s_{\text{total}}$, which raises $\sigma$ and offsets the potential convergence benefit.

\textbf{On CIFAR-10, the effect reverses.} At $\varepsilon=1$, accuracy drops from $0.178$ to $0.134$ to $0.101$ as local work increases, well beyond the $6.38\%$ noise floor. The peak round falls from 13 to 4, while post-peak energy waste increases from $2596$~J to $14819$~J.

\textbf{Clipping affects convergence, not computation.} Changing $C$ leaves per-round energy essentially unchanged on both datasets, but it can affect convergence, particularly on CIFAR-10. For example, at $C=2$ it peaks at $0.191$ but falls to $0.102$ by the end, losing $46.5\%$ of its peak accuracy. Thus, the fixed $C=1.0$ used in our main experiments is conservative rather than optimal.

\subsection{Carbon Cost by Grid}

Table~\ref{tab:carbon} and Fig.~\ref{fig:carbon} scale the measured HAR
energy to 1000 runs and apply published grid carbon intensities~\cite{energy2024yearly} (2025 release), data year 2024; the scaling repeats the measured workload
rather than extrapolating to other hardware. The cost attributable to privacy
ranges from $138$--$192$~gCO$_2$eq on Algeria's gas-dominated grid to
$6.4$--$9.0$~g in Norway, a factor of $21.4$ for the same computation and the
same guarantee. Selecting a privacy budget is therefore partly a geographic
decision, a consideration absent from the privacy-utility framing.
