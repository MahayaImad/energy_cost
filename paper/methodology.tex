\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Accounting Model}

We enforce \emph{client-level local DP} with an untrusted server: each client
holds its own $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ budget over the entire federated run,
accounted over every local SGD step it takes in every round it
joins~\cite{geyer} --- not per round, and not per local epoch. For a client
with $n$ local training examples,
\begin{equation}
s_{\text{total}} = R \cdot q_c \cdot E \cdot \lceil n / B \rceil
\label{eq:steps}
\end{equation}
with $R$ rounds, client sampling fraction $q_c$, $E$ local epochs and batch
size $B$. We invert the RDP accountant~\cite{rdp} once, before training, to
find the noise multiplier $\sigma$ that spends $\varepsilon$ at $\delta$ after
$s_{\text{total}}$ steps, and hold $\sigma$ constant for the run.

We do not use the privacy engine's running accountant: in simulation each
client is rebuilt every round, so an engine-internal accountant resets every
round and reports a per-round $\varepsilon$ while the true budget compounds.
We claim no amplification by client subsampling, which would need secure
aggregation or a shuffler. The clipping norm $C$ is fixed across all budgets,
since tuning it per $\varepsilon$ would confound the comparison.

\subsection{What the Reported $\varepsilon$ Actually Bounds}
\label{sec:spent}

Equation~\ref{eq:steps} uses the \emph{expected} number of rounds a client
joins, $R \cdot q_c$. The realised number is a binomial draw around it: at
$R = 100$ and $q_c = 0.5$ the expectation is 50 with a standard deviation of
5, so a client sampled 60 times takes $20\%$ more steps than the accountant
was paid for. The assurance that a reported $\varepsilon$ is an upper bound
rests on claiming no subsampling amplification and says nothing about
over-participation.

We therefore recompute each client's spent budget afterwards, from the
participation logged every round, partition sizes rebuilt deterministically,
and the same accountant. Participation ranged over $33$--$64$ rounds on HAR
and $8$--$22$ on CIFAR-10, and \textbf{all 29 private runs exceed their stated
budget}, by $4.1\%$ to $22.3\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:perround}). A study reporting
client-level DP in simulation should either report the worst realised budget,
as we do, or calibrate $\sigma$ against a participation bound.

\subsection{Measuring Energy}

GPU energy comes from NVML's
\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetTotalEnergyConsumption}, a hardware counter in
accumulated millijoules~\cite{nvml}, differenced around each round: measured
rather than modelled from a TDP figure, and free of the integration error of
power sampling. The window runs from dispatch of the round's training messages
to the end of aggregation, covering everything the round costs on the device.
An idle baseline probed over a quiet five-second window, after waiting for the
board to settle, is reported and subtracted --- $17.93$~W (sd $0.12$) on the
HAR sweep --- over the window the counter actually covers rather than over
whole-process wall-clock, which would over-subtract by roughly $40\%$.

Deriving $\sigma$ is kept out of that window entirely. Inverting the
accountant binary-searches it, and the search costs far more for a loose
budget than a tight one --- $0.086$~s at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ against
$0.693$~s at $\varepsilon = 8$. Called once per client per round inside the
measured region, as the natural implementation does, this alone reproduced an
entire wall-clock trend across $\varepsilon$ while executed step counts
stayed flat: an accountant artefact shaped exactly like a cost of privacy. We
derive every $\sigma$ the sweep needs before any measurement begins.

\subsection{Verification Before Interpretation}

The energy axis means nothing unless every budget ran the same workload, so a
gate runs before any number is quoted. It checks that each condition has all
its seeds; that wall-clock is flat across the private conditions; that
executed optimiser steps match the count the accountant was calibrated
against; that every client trained on the GPU the counter is watching, since a
silent fallback to CPU would record the energy of an idle card; and that idle
baselines agree across runs, which caught one probed while the board was still
busy at $300$~W. Both floors are computed from the across-seed spread rather
than assumed. Run order is randomised so drift over a sweep can be told apart
from a budget effect: on HAR, wall-clock correlates with execution order at
$\rho = -0.154$ and with $\varepsilon$ at $\rho = -0.098$, the two predictors
themselves decorrelated at $\rho = -0.196$.

\subsection{Setup}

UCI-HAR~\cite{uci-har} gives 21 clients, one per training participant of the
published subject-disjoint split; the nine test participants are held out, so
central accuracy measures generalisation to people the federation never saw.
Each client splits its windows 80/20 \emph{within each activity class} ---
necessary because the activities are recorded in blocks and the windows
overlap by $50\%$, so an ordered cut leaves clients training on a subset of
the classes. The model is a three-block 1D CNN over the nine raw inertial
channels ($37$k parameters, no batch normalisation, which per-sample gradients
forbid). CIFAR-10 gives 20 clients partitioned by
Dirichlet($\alpha = 0.5$)~\cite{dirichlet} and a $62$k-parameter CNN.

Both sweeps use $\varepsilon \in \{0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, \infty\}$ with three seeds,
$\delta = 10^{-5}$, $q_c = 0.5$, $B = 32$, learning rate $0.01$, one local
epoch and $C = 1.0$; HAR runs 100 rounds and CIFAR-10 runs 30, simulated with
Flower~\cite{flower} on one NVIDIA RTX 5080 (PyTorch 2.10, CUDA 12.8). We do
not enable deterministic kernels: they would change which kernels run and
therefore what we measure, so reproducibility comes from averaging seeds.
