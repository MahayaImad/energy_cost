\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Federated Setup}

We use two datasets, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:setup}. Both run on the
Flower framework~\cite{flower} with FedAvg aggregation, $50\%$ of clients
sampled for training and $50\%$ for evaluation each round, one local epoch of
SGD per round (batch size 32, learning rate 0.01, momentum 0.9), and three
seeds per configuration.

\textbf{UCI-HAR is the primary dataset.} It carries the headline claims
because the client boundary is a real person and the task is the
sensor-on-edge setting the study is about. The data was collected from 30
volunteers wearing a waist-mounted smartphone, and we keep the published
subject-disjoint split: the 21 training participants become the federation's
clients, one client per person, and the 9 test participants are held out
entirely. Central accuracy therefore measures generalisation to people the
federation never saw. \emph{Clients correspond to individual study
participants}, so the partition is given by who contributed the data rather
than by a synthetic heterogeneity parameter --- which is the arrangement a
paper about protecting individuals' data should be measuring.

We train on the raw inertial signals (nine channels of body and total
acceleration and angular velocity, 128 samples per $2.56$~s window) rather
than the 561 engineered features, since a network over raw sensor channels is
what an edge deployment would run. The model is a three-block 1D CNN with
global average pooling ($37$k parameters, $0.15$~MB), close enough to the
CIFAR-10 model's $62$k that per-round energy remains comparable between the
two datasets. Neither model uses batch normalisation, which Opacus rejects
because it mixes information across the samples in a batch.

Local train and validation sets are split \emph{within each activity class},
in file order, discarding the window that straddles the cut. Two constraints
require this. HAR windows overlap by $50\%$, so a random split would put the
two halves of one window on opposite sides of the train/validation line; and a
participant records the six activities in blocks, so a single ordered cut
across the whole recording would hand the earlier activities to one side and
the later ones to the other, leaving clients training on a subset of the
classes. Splitting within each class satisfies both.

We train HAR for 100 rounds. The non-private baseline reaches $95\%$ of its
final accuracy at round 59 and $99\%$ at round 86, so a shorter budget would
not distinguish convergence from truncation --- and the round budget is not a
free parameter here, since it enters the privacy accounting directly
(Section~\ref{sec:accounting}).

\textbf{CIFAR-10 is secondary}, and serves to check the same effects on a
harder benchmark. A five-layer convolutional network ($62$k parameters,
$0.24$~MB) is trained across 20 simulated clients over 30 rounds, with data
partitioned by a Dirichlet distribution over labels at $\alpha=0.5$, the
standard non-IID setting~\cite{dirichlet}.

Under both partitions the per-client dataset sizes differ, which matters for
privacy accounting below.

\subsection{Privacy Accounting}
\label{sec:accounting}

We enforce \emph{client-level local differential privacy}: each client holds
its own $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ budget over the entire federated run, and the
server is untrusted.

The budget is accounted over all local SGD steps a client takes across all
rounds in which it participates --- not per round, and not per local epoch.
For a client with $n$ local training examples, batch size $B$, $R$ rounds,
client sampling fraction $q_c$ and $E$ local epochs:
%
\begin{align}
  s_{\text{epoch}} &= \lceil n / B \rceil \\
  s_{\text{total}} &= R \, q_c \, E \, s_{\text{epoch}}
\end{align}
%
We invert the RDP accountant~\cite{rdp} once, before training starts, to find
the noise multiplier $\sigma$ that yields the target $\varepsilon$ at $\delta$
after $s_{\text{total}}$ steps, and hold $\sigma$ constant for the run.

Precomputation is deliberate. In simulation each client object is
reconstructed every round, so an engine-internal accountant resets each round
and reports a per-round $\varepsilon$ while the true budget compounds ---
understating the privacy loss by roughly a factor of the round count.

Two properties of the sampling deserve statement. First, Opacus'
\texttt{DPDataLoader} samples at rate $1/\lceil n/B \rceil$ rather than $B/n$;
we calibrate at the rate the sampler actually uses, so the realised
$\varepsilon$ matches the target to within the accountant's search tolerance
rather than being conservative by an unstated margin. Second, we claim
\textbf{no privacy amplification by client subsampling}: amplification
requires secure aggregation or a shuffler, neither of which we assume. The
reported $\varepsilon$ is therefore an upper bound.

Because per-client dataset sizes differ under both partitions, $\sigma$
differs per client; Table~\ref{tab:sigma} reports the observed ranges.

The two datasets sit in very different accounting regimes, and the HAR values
do not follow from the CIFAR-10 ones. A HAR client holds roughly $279$
training windows against CIFAR-10's $2000$, so the sampling rate
$1/\lceil n/B \rceil$ is about seven times higher, and that outweighs the
smaller number of steps per round. Combined with the longer round budget that
HAR's convergence requires, $\sigma$ at $\varepsilon=0.5$ reaches $17.5$--$20.6$
on HAR against $2.8$--$6.1$ on CIFAR-10. Under local DP the round budget is
therefore not free: extending training to reach convergence raises $\sigma$
for every round, so the two objectives are in direct tension.

\subsection{Energy Measurement}

Energy is read from the NVML hardware counter
\texttt{nvmlDeviceGetTotalEnergyConsumption}~\cite{nvml}, which accumulates
millijoules on the device. We difference the counter before and after each
round, so the measurement involves no power sampling and no numerical
integration, and cannot miss a transient. This is a measured quantity, not a
TDP-based estimate.

Measurement is server-side and per round, bracketing \texttt{configure\_train}
through \texttt{aggregate\_evaluate}. In simulation all clients share one GPU,
so a round-level window captures the full cost regardless of how the runtime
schedules client actors; measuring inside each client would double-count
concurrent work.

We report energy both gross and net of an idle baseline. The baseline is
sampled per run over a quiet-GPU window, after waiting for board power to
stabilise: a card does not reach its floor the moment a run ends, and probing
during that tail reads a still-busy baseline. Because net energy subtracts
$\text{idle} \times t$, an inflated baseline silently understates one run's
net energy without touching any other measurement. The effect is easy to hit
with short runs --- back-to-back HAR runs 35~s apart once produced a
$29.5$~W baseline where neighbouring conditions read $18.6$--$19.9$~W --- so
we also compare every run's baseline against the median across the sweep and
discard runs that deviate. The median across the HAR sweep is $18.21$~W.

Net energy subtracts the baseline over the wall-clock the counter actually
covers, which is \emph{not} whole-process wall-clock: run start-up,
centralised evaluation between rounds, and teardown all fall outside the round
windows. Subtracting idle over the longer interval would understate net
compute energy substantially.

Repeating each configuration across three seeds gives a measurement noise
floor of $\pm 5.43$~W ($9.46\%$) on HAR and $\pm 4.38$~W ($6.38\%$) on
CIFAR-10. We report no effect smaller than this as an effect. The HAR floor is
the wider of the two because its rounds are short (about $0.48$~s): GPU power
varies over tens of milliseconds, so a round lasting a fraction of a second
averages little of that away. The joules are counted correctly either way, but
per-round energy is a noisier estimate on HAR than on CIFAR-10, and whole-run
totals are the more reliable quantity there.

\subsection{Experimental Controls}

\textbf{Fixed clipping norm.} $C=1.0$ for every $\varepsilon$. Tuning $C$ per
budget would confound the privacy--utility comparison. Our ablation shows
$C$ affects convergence substantially while leaving per-round energy
unchanged, so $C=1.0$ is a conservative rather than optimal choice.

\textbf{Identical compute across budgets.} The energy axis is only
interpretable if every $\varepsilon$ condition executes the same workload. We
verify per round that executed optimiser steps match the steps the accountant
was calibrated for: on HAR all six conditions execute 89 steps per round, and
executed and calibrated counts agree exactly. Opacus' loader yields exactly $\lceil n/B \rceil$ batches
per epoch, so step counts are deterministic and independent of
$\varepsilon$; Poisson sampling randomises batch \emph{size} (approximately
$\pm 2\%$ of $n$), not step count, and that residual is unbiased with respect
to $\varepsilon$. Across the sweep, executed and calibrated steps agree to
within one step per round ($0.16\%$), a floating-point artefact of the
loader's batch-count computation that runs \emph{fewer} steps than accounted,
preserving the upper-bound claim.

\textbf{Accountant excluded from the measurement window.} Deriving $\sigma$
requires a binary search over the RDP accountant whose cost depends strongly
on the target budget: we measure $0.086$~s at $\varepsilon=0.5$ against
$0.693$~s at $\varepsilon=8$. Executed inside the federated loop, this cost
falls within the energy-measurement window and scales with $\varepsilon$,
producing a wall-clock trend that is indistinguishable in shape from a cost of
privacy. In our first clean sweep it accounted for the entire measured
$\varepsilon$ trend (predicted $+18.2$~s at $\varepsilon=8$ relative to
$\varepsilon=0.5$; measured $+19.7$~s) while step counts stayed flat. We
therefore derive all $\sigma$ values before measurement begins. We flag this
as a general hazard for energy studies of privacy mechanisms.

\textbf{Randomised run order.} Sweeping $\varepsilon$ in a fixed order makes
execution order and $\varepsilon$ rank-correlated ($\rho=-0.99$), so any drift
over a sweep is inseparable from a privacy effect. We randomise the order of
all 18 runs, reducing the correlation to $\rho=-0.20$ and making the
$\varepsilon$ trend attributable.

\textbf{Determinism deliberately not enforced.} We do not enable
\texttt{torch.use\_deterministic\_algorithms}: it forces slower kernel
selection and would change measured energy, i.e.\ we would be measuring a
configuration nobody deploys. Reproducibility comes from averaging over seeds.
Per-client step counts are deterministic; the round-to-round variation in
aggregate step count comes from client sampling, which we record per round.

\subsection{Limitations}

Clients are virtual and share a single GPU rather than running on distinct
devices; on HAR each virtual client holds one real participant's data, so the
partition is authentic even though the execution is not. Relative comparisons between privacy budgets hold; absolute
per-device joules do not transfer to real edge hardware. CPU RAPL counters
were unavailable in our containerised environment, so measurement is GPU-only
and communication energy is modelled analytically rather than measured. The
workload does not saturate the device (roughly 60--65~W on a 360~W card),
which is why idle-subtracted figures are reported alongside gross ones.

Two choices specific to HAR deserve statement. Per-channel standardisation
constants are computed once from the training split; they are global across
clients, so strictly a federated deployment would have to agree them without
pooling raw data. We treat them as a fixed preprocessing constant rather than
a learned quantity. And the round budget is set from the non-private
convergence curve, which is favourable to the baseline: the private conditions
inherit a budget longer than any of them needs, and consequently a larger
$\sigma$ than a budget tuned to each would give. Reporting every condition at
one budget is the control that makes the energy comparison interpretable, but
it means the private accuracies here are a lower bound on what a
per-$\varepsilon$ tuned round budget would achieve.
