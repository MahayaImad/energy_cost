\section{Conclusion}

We measured what differential privacy costs in energy when added to federated
learning, using hardware counters around each round on two datasets, with
UCI-HAR partitioned so that each client holds one study participant's data.

The cost divides in two. A fixed per-round overhead of $+45\%$ on HAR and
$+60\%$ on CIFAR-10, flat in $\varepsilon$ within our resolution, comes from
per-sample gradient computation and clipping. Everything else acts through the
number of rounds required, and it is the larger term: reaching $0.70$ accuracy
on HAR costs $5.8\times$ the non-private energy at $\varepsilon=4$, and is
unreachable at $\varepsilon \le 2$ regardless of expenditure. The two respond
to different interventions, which is why separating them matters more than
quantifying either.

Three practical rules follow. The round budget should be derived from the
privacy budget rather than from the task, since rounds consume budget and
training to convergence is self-defeating under tight privacy --- at
$\varepsilon=0.5$ on HAR, $83\%$ of a run's energy is spent after the accuracy
peak. The privacy budget and the accuracy requirement should be chosen
together rather than in sequence, because a budget implies a quality ceiling
that no amount of computation reaches. And when the private model is
noise-limited, hyperparameter search is not the lever: on HAR at
$\varepsilon=1$, varying local epochs or the clipping norm moved accuracy by
under $3\%$ against a $9.46\%$ noise floor.

Because the cost is measured in joules, it inherits the carbon intensity of
wherever training runs: the privacy premium on 1000 HAR runs is $18.8$ times
larger on Algeria's grid than on Norway's, for identical computation and an
identical guarantee. This is a dimension of privacy budget selection that a
privacy--utility evaluation cannot represent, and it is particularly awkward
for federated learning, where the computation is distributed across whatever
grids the participating devices sit on.

We also report a measurement hazard. Instrumentation attached to a privacy
mechanism tends to scale with the privacy parameter, so its cost mimics the
effect under study; the accountant's noise-calibration search alone reproduced
the entire $\varepsilon$ trend in one of our sweeps. Deriving the noise
multipliers before measurement, verifying per round that executed and
calibrated step counts agree, and randomising run order are cheap controls
that we recommend for any energy study of a privacy mechanism.

Two limits bound what we claim. Clients are virtual and share one GPU, so the
ratios reported here transfer to real devices but the absolute joules do not;
confirming them on physical hardware is the immediate next step. And our two
datasets bound the effects rather than establishing them --- the per-round
overhead and the quality ceiling appear on both, while the stopping round and
the sensitivity to hyperparameters each appear on one. Characterising where
the boundary between those regimes falls, and whether it is set by task
difficulty, by the magnitude of the noise, or by their ratio, is the question
this work leaves open.
