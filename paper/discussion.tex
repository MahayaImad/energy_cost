\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Two Costs, Two Remedies}

The energy cost of differential privacy separates into a fixed per-round
overhead and a convergence penalty, and the two call for different responses.

The overhead is an implementation cost: per-sample gradient computation and
gradient clipping, paid identically whether the budget is tight or loose. We
measure it at $+45\%$ on HAR and $+60\%$ on CIFAR-10, flat in $\varepsilon$
within the noise floor of both. It is the part a systems contribution can
attack --- through better per-sample gradient kernels, or by amortising the
clipping --- and the part that scales down as those implementations improve.

The convergence penalty is not addressable that way. It arises because a
noisier update carries less information, so more rounds are needed for the
same accuracy, and it is bounded by the privacy accounting rather than by any
implementation. Reporting energy per round, as an efficiency-focused
evaluation naturally would, sees only the first cost and concludes that
privacy is a flat surcharge of roughly half again. Measured against the
accuracy a deployment actually needs, the second cost is several times larger.
This is the practical reason the third axis is worth adding: it does not
merely rank configurations, it changes which engineering effort is worth
spending.

\subsection{Privacy Sets a Quality Ceiling, Not Just a Price}

The more consequential finding is that the cost of privacy is not a constant
multiplier. On HAR it is invisible at $0.40$ accuracy, where every condition
costs the same within noise; it is $1.17$--$2.10\times$ at $0.50$; it is
$5.8\times$ at $0.70$, with $\varepsilon \le 2$ never arriving; and at $0.85$,
which the non-private model reaches in $878$~J, no budget we tested arrives at
any expenditure.

A privacy budget therefore implies a maximum achievable accuracy, and past
that point the trade is not energy for accuracy but nothing for nothing. This
reframes budget selection. The usual formulation --- choose $\varepsilon$,
then see what utility follows --- treats the two as sequential. Our results
suggest choosing them jointly: a deployment that needs $0.85$ activity
recognition accuracy cannot have $\varepsilon = 1$ under this accounting, and
discovering that after provisioning hardware is expensive. The energy axis is
what makes the ceiling visible, because a utility-only evaluation reports a
lower number without indicating whether more computation would fix it.

\subsection{The Round Budget Is Part of the Privacy Budget}

Under client-level local DP the number of rounds enters the accounting
directly: every round a client participates in consumes budget, so $\sigma$ is
fixed before training starts by a decision that federated learning usually
treats as a free parameter. On HAR, extending the budget from 30 to 100 rounds
--- which the non-private model needs, reaching $95\%$ of its final accuracy
only at round 59 --- raises $\sigma$ at $\varepsilon=0.5$ from $10.2$ to
$18.4$. Training to convergence is self-defeating: the extension is what
prevents convergence.

The consequence is a stopping rule. Under a sufficiently tight budget the
accuracy curve peaks and declines, and training past the peak spends energy
while losing accuracy. At $\varepsilon=0.5$ on HAR, $83\%$ of the run's energy
falls after the peak. CIFAR-10 shows the effect across more budgets, with the
peak round ordering monotonically from 7 to 24 as $\varepsilon$ loosens.

We state this conditionally, since HAR reproduces it only at its tightest
budget while CIFAR-10 reproduces it across four. The mechanism is the same in
both --- accumulated noise eventually dominates the aggregated update --- but
where the crossover falls relative to the round budget depends on the task,
and one of our two datasets does not exhibit it at $\varepsilon \ge 1$. What
generalises is the direction: the round budget should be derived from the
privacy budget, and a deployment that sizes it from the non-private
convergence curve risks paying for rounds that make the model worse.

\subsection{When the Model Is Noise-Limited, Tuning Is Not the Lever}

Our HAR ablations are negative and useful. At $\varepsilon=1$, neither the
number of local epochs nor the clipping norm moves accuracy: both span under
$3\%$ against a $9.46\%$ noise floor. With $\sigma$ near $10$, the injected
noise dominates what these settings contribute.

The practical reading is that hyperparameter search is a poor use of compute
in this regime. A practitioner who responds to a disappointing private model
by sweeping learning rates, clipping norms and local epochs will spend
considerable energy to land within noise of where they started. The levers
that remain are the privacy budget and the round budget.

This does not generalise to every regime, and CIFAR-10 shows why. There the
private model retains more signal, the same settings matter a great deal, and
they can be got badly wrong: five local epochs costs $3.7\times$ the energy per
round and loses $43\%$ of the accuracy that one epoch achieves, while
post-peak waste grows from $2596$~J to $14819$~J. Increasing local work per
round is the standard way to reduce communication in federated learning; under
DP it stops paying on HAR and actively harms on CIFAR-10, because local steps
enter the budget and force a larger $\sigma$. The boundary between the two
regimes --- where noise begins to dominate tuning --- is worth characterising
and we have only bracketed it.

\subsection{Where the Computation Runs}

Because the cost of privacy is measured in joules, it inherits the carbon
intensity of wherever those joules are drawn. The same $\varepsilon$, bought
with the same computation, costs $105$--$133$~gCO$_2$eq per 1000 HAR training
runs on Algeria's gas-dominated grid and $5.6$--$7.1$~g in Norway, a factor of
$18.8$.

Privacy budget selection is therefore partly a siting decision, which the
privacy--utility framing has no place for. Two deployments with identical
regulatory requirements and identical models carry environmental costs an
order of magnitude apart. For federated learning specifically the point is
sharper than for centralised training, since the computation is distributed
across wherever the participating devices happen to be, and no single operator
chooses the grid. A deployment cannot straightforwardly relocate its
computation to a cleaner grid the way a data centre operator can; what it can
adjust is how much computation the privacy requirement demands.

\subsection{Measuring Energy Around a Privacy Accountant}

One methodological result deserves separate statement because it would affect
any study of this kind.

Deriving $\sigma$ requires a search over the privacy accountant, and that
search costs more for a loose budget than a tight one: we measure $0.086$~s at
$\varepsilon=0.5$ against $0.693$~s at $\varepsilon=8$. Run inside the
federated loop, as the natural implementation does since each client needs its
own $\sigma$, this cost falls inside the energy-measurement window and scales
with $\varepsilon$. It is then indistinguishable in shape from a cost of
privacy. In our first clean sweep it accounted for the entire observed
wall-clock trend: predicted $+18.2$~s at $\varepsilon=8$ relative to
$\varepsilon=0.5$, measured $+19.7$~s, while executed step counts stayed flat.

The general hazard is that instrumentation attached to the privacy mechanism
scales with the privacy parameter, so its cost mimics the effect under study.
An evaluation that measures wall-clock or energy around a privacy accountant
will find the accountant's own cost scaling with the budget, and nothing in
the resulting numbers announces that. We derive all $\sigma$ values before
measurement begins, verify per round that executed and calibrated step counts
agree, and randomise run order so that drift over a sweep cannot be mistaken
for an $\varepsilon$ effect --- sweeping $\varepsilon$ in a fixed order leaves
the two correlated at $\rho = -0.99$. We recommend all three for future work
on the energy of privacy mechanisms.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

Clients are virtual and share one GPU. Relative comparisons between privacy
budgets hold, but absolute per-device joules do not transfer to real edge
hardware, and confirming the ratios on physical devices is the natural next
step. Measurement is GPU-only, since CPU RAPL counters were unavailable in our
environment, and communication energy is modelled rather than measured; for a
$0.15$--$0.24$~MB model over tens of rounds we expect it to be small relative
to computation, but we have not shown that.

Two datasets bound the effects rather than establishing them. The per-round
overhead and the quality ceiling appear on both; the stopping round and the
hyperparameter sensitivity appear on one each. Whether the boundary between
those regimes is set by task difficulty, by $\sigma$, or by the ratio between
them is open.

Finally, our accounting claims no amplification by subsampling, so the
reported $\varepsilon$ is an upper bound. A deployment with secure aggregation
or a shuffler would obtain the same utility at a smaller reported budget, and
the energy figures here should be read as the cost of privacy under the
weakest trust assumptions rather than the best achievable.
